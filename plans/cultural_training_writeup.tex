\begin{abstract}
We present a two-stage pipeline for improving the cultural understanding of language models through figurative language. Our approach currently targets English and Chinese, with planned extensions to Hindi and Arabic, by leveraging large-scale idiom datasets ($\sim$21K English, $\sim$28K Chinese idioms with figurative and literal meanings). In the first stage, we filter and deduplicate the C4/mC4 corpus to extract high-quality documents containing culturally meaningful idioms, cap per-idiom document frequency to ensure broad coverage, and augment documents with idiom metadata tags exposing literal and figurative meanings. In the second stage, we generate instruction tuning data that trains the model to apply idioms in context, recognize shared figurative meanings within and across languages, and reason about the cultural significance of recurring symbolic entities. We describe the data construction methodology for both stages, discuss our plans for extending the pipeline to Hindi and Arabic, and outline our evaluation plan on cultural benchmarks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Language models trained on large web corpora acquire broad linguistic competence but often lack deep understanding of culture-specific behaviors, norms, and values. Figurative language, including idioms, proverbs, and metaphorical expressions, encodes cultural knowledge in ways that literal text does not. An idiom's figurative meaning frequently reflects historical attitudes, social norms, and philosophical worldviews unique to its culture of origin. For instance, the Chinese chengyu ``pao zhuan yin yu'' (throw a brick to attract jade) encodes a Confucian ethic of humility and collective improvement, while the English idiom ``pull yourself up by your bootstraps'' reflects an Anglo-American emphasis on individual self-reliance. These figurative meanings are cultural artifacts, and a model that understands them gains access to a layer of cultural reasoning that surface-level language competence alone cannot provide.

We propose a two-stage training pipeline that systematically injects such cultural knowledge into language models. The first stage, continue pretraining, exposes the model to a large volume of natural text containing idioms in context, augmented with explicit meaning annotations. The second stage, instruction tuning, trains the model to reason about idiom usage, cross-lingual figurative parallels, and the cultural significance of symbolic entities that recur across a language's idiom inventory. Our pipeline operates independently per language (English prompts and idioms for English culture, Chinese prompts and chengyu for Chinese culture) to avoid cross-cultural contamination and ensure each language's cultural knowledge is learned on its own terms. While our current implementation covers English and Chinese, the pipeline is designed to be language-agnostic, and we plan to extend it to Hindi and Arabic, two languages with rich figurative traditions and distinct cultural value systems.

\section{Data Resources}

Our pipeline draws on two primary data sources. The first is a pair of comprehensive idiom datasets: approximately 21K English idioms with figurative meanings, sourced from multiple dictionaries and filtered to retain only entries with non-trivial figurative content, and approximately 28K Chinese chengyu with figurative meanings and classical source citations. Each idiom entry contains the idiom text, a list of key conceptual entities, literal meanings (often with classical source quotations for Chinese), and figurative meanings. Trivial English entries, such as single common words, basic grammatical constructions, and phrases understood literally without cultural context, are removed using a combination of heuristic filtering (requiring non-empty figurative meanings) and LLM-based classification.

The second data source is the C4 corpus~\citep{raffel2020exploring} (English: $\sim$365M documents) and its multilingual counterpart mC4 (Chinese subset), which serve as the raw material for continue pretraining.

\section{Continue Pretraining}

The goal of continue pretraining is to expose the model to a large volume of natural text in which idioms appear in authentic contexts, while ensuring the model can associate each idiom with its figurative meaning. This stage involves three components: document filtering, deduplication with frequency capping, and idiom metadata tagging.

\paragraph{Document filtering.} We filter the C4/mC4 corpus to extract documents containing at least one idiom from our dataset. We stream the full corpus and use Aho-Corasick multi-pattern matching to test each document against all $\sim$21K English or $\sim$28K Chinese idiom patterns simultaneously in a single pass, with case-insensitive matching for English. Given the scale of the English C4 corpus ($\sim$365M documents), we partition the workload across parallel jobs for efficient processing. During filtering, we also accumulate per-idiom document counts that record how many documents contain each idiom, which feed into the subsequent deduplication and frequency capping stage. Additionally, our pipeline supports an infini-gram~\citep{liu2024infinigram} mode that indexes the filtered corpus and enables efficient per-idiom frequency counting and targeted document extraction without re-scanning the full dataset.

\paragraph{Deduplication and frequency capping.} A naive filtering approach produces a heavily skewed distribution: common idioms containing high-frequency words (e.g., ``hand,'' ``heart,'' ``time'' in English, or ``xin'' (heart), ``ren'' (person), ``tian'' (sky) in Chinese) appear in hundreds of thousands of documents, while rare idioms may appear in only a handful. Training on this skewed distribution would cause the model to over-attend to common idioms while neglecting rare, culturally specific ones. To address this, we perform per-idiom frequency capping at a maximum of 10,000 documents per idiom. When an idiom appears in more than 10,000 documents, we select a high-quality subset by prioritizing documents where the idiom appears in meaningful context (not in lists, dictionary entries, or metadata), the document is of sufficient length to provide contextual richness, and the document is not a near-duplicate of other selected documents. By capping frequent idioms while retaining all documents for rare ones, we ensure broad and balanced coverage across the full idiom vocabulary.

\paragraph{Idiom metadata tagging.} A key component of our continue pretraining approach is augmenting filtered documents with structured idiom metadata. When a document contains one or more idioms from our dataset, we append a knowledge block to the document text that lists each detected idiom along with its figurative and literal meanings from our idiom dataset. For Chinese chengyu, this includes classical source citations. This tagging creates explicit meaning associations: the model learns to connect each idiom with its figurative meaning through co-occurrence in the training data, rather than relying solely on contextual inference.

\section{Instruction Tuning}

After continue pretraining, we fine-tune the model on instruction-following data that requires idiom understanding and cultural reasoning. We construct four complementary types of training examples from our idiom datasets, each targeting a different facet of cultural-figurative competence. All examples are generated by prompting an LLM with the relevant idiom entries (including figurative and literal meanings) and task-specific instructions, then validated for well-formedness.

\paragraph{Type 1: Contextual idiom application.} For each idiom in the dataset, we use an LLM to generate a realistic scenario grounded in the idiom's figurative meaning, such as a situation, behavior, or cultural context in which the idiom would naturally apply. The training question presents this scenario and asks the model to identify an appropriate idiom and explain why it fits. For example, given the Chinese chengyu ``pao zhuan yin yu'' (throw a brick to attract jade), whose figurative meaning is to offer a modest contribution in hopes of eliciting better ideas from others, the LLM generates a scenario such as a junior colleague presenting a rough proposal at a team meeting to encourage senior members to share their refined insights. The expected output identifies the idiom, applies it to the scenario, and explains the cultural reasoning (in this case, the Confucian value of humility and collective improvement) that makes the idiom apt. This type trains the model to connect figurative meanings to concrete cultural contexts.

\paragraph{Type 2: Similar-meaning idiom clustering.} We group idioms within each language that share overlapping figurative meanings, such as English idioms expressing perseverance such as ``keep your chin up,'' ``hang in there,'' and ``when the going gets tough, the tough get going.'' For each group, we generate questions that present one idiom and ask the model to identify other idioms with similar figurative meanings, then explain the shared cultural logic and any nuanced differences between them. This type trains the model to recognize semantic relationships across the idiom inventory and to articulate what cultural themes or values unify a cluster of expressions.

\paragraph{Type 3: Cross-lingual idiom comparison.} We identify idioms across languages that express related figurative concepts, such as the English ``every cloud has a silver lining'' and the Chinese ``sai weng shi ma'' (the old man at the frontier lost his horse), both conveying optimism in adversity. We construct two sub-tasks from such pairs. In the first, the model is given an idiom in one language and asked to find one or more idioms in another language that convey a related meaning, explaining the conceptual overlap. In the second, the model is given a pair of cross-lingual idioms and asked to analyze the cultural differences they reveal, for example, how the English expression frames optimism as an inherent property of the situation (the cloud \textit{has} a silver lining), while the Chinese proverb frames it through a narrative of reversal and Daoist acceptance of fortune's impermanence. This type trains the model to reason about both cross-cultural commonalities and culture-specific divergences in how figurative language encodes values.

\paragraph{Type 4: Entity-level cultural significance.} Many idioms across a language's inventory share recurring entities, such as ``heart'' in English and ``long'' (dragon) in Chinese, that carry cultural associations beyond any single idiom. We identify high-frequency entities across each language's idiom dataset and generate questions that probe their broader cultural significance. For example: ``What cultural meanings does the dragon carry in Chinese idioms, and how do these differ from its connotations in English?'' The expected output surveys how the entity functions across multiple idioms and what cultural values or beliefs it encodes. Where an entity appears in both languages' idiom sets, we additionally generate cross-lingual comparison questions. This type differs from Type~3 in that it targets the cultural symbolism of shared entities rather than the figurative meanings of complete idioms, training the model to reason about the deeper cultural building blocks from which figurative language is constructed.

\section{Evaluation Plan}

We plan to evaluate the trained model on several cultural benchmarks to assess whether the two-stage pipeline improves culture-related understanding and reasoning. These include cultural natural language inference tasks requiring cultural knowledge for correct entailment judgments; the World Value Survey, which tests alignment with cultural value distributions across different populations; direct evaluation of figurative meaning comprehension in both languages, measuring whether the model can correctly identify and explain the cultural significance of idioms encountered in novel contexts; and held-out instruction tuning examples from each of the four types described above, measuring the model's ability to apply idioms in context, cluster related idioms, draw cross-lingual parallels, and reason about entity-level cultural symbolism.

\section{Extension to Hindi and Arabic}

While our current implementation focuses on English and Chinese, the pipeline is designed to generalize to additional languages. We plan to extend coverage to Hindi and Arabic, two languages with rich figurative traditions and cultural contexts that are underrepresented in current language model training. Hindi possesses a wealth of muhavare (idioms) and lokoktiyan (proverbs) that encode South Asian cultural values around family hierarchy, dharma, and social obligation, while Arabic amthal (proverbs) and kinayat (figurative expressions) reflect cultural norms rooted in Islamic tradition, tribal honor codes, and the centrality of hospitality.

For both languages, the extension follows the same methodology. First, we will compile idiom datasets with figurative and literal meanings from established dictionaries and linguistic resources. Second, we will filter the corresponding mC4 language subsets using the same Aho-Corasick matching and per-idiom frequency capping strategy. Third, we will generate instruction tuning data following the same four-type taxonomy (contextual application, similar-meaning clustering, cross-lingual comparison, and entity-level cultural significance), with prompts written natively in each language. The language-agnostic design of our pipeline means that adding a new language requires only the idiom dataset, the mC4 subset, and native-language prompt templates, with no changes to the core infrastructure.

\section{Conclusion}

We have presented a two-stage pipeline for cultural alignment of language models through figurative language. The continue pretraining stage provides broad, balanced exposure to idioms in natural context with explicit meaning annotations, while the instruction tuning stage trains the model to apply idioms in context, recognize shared figurative meanings within and across languages, and reason about the cultural significance of recurring symbolic entities. By operating independently per language, capping per-idiom document frequency for balanced coverage, tagging documents with structured idiom metadata, and generating four complementary types of instruction data targeting different facets of cultural-figurative competence, our approach provides a principled methodology for injecting cultural knowledge into language models. With English and Chinese as our initial targets and planned extensions to Hindi and Arabic, the pipeline aims to improve cultural understanding across a diverse set of linguistic and cultural traditions.
